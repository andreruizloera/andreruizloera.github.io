Piranesi

Susanna Clarke · 2020 · Read 2026

Funny enough, I first heard about Piranesi through TikTok. Jack Edwards was interviewing Matt Damon and Anne Hathaway for The Odyssey and gave each of them a book. Anne Hathaway got Fruit Fly by Josh Silver, while Matt Damon got Piranesi. I remember the comments joking that Matt Damon looked jealous of Anne Hathaway’s book, and something about the whole interaction made me curious about what exactly he had been handed. Edwards also said that Piranesi was one of those books you should read knowing basically nothing about it. Everything else I saw online said the same thing. People seemed incredibly divided on the book, it was either boring as hell or one of the greatest things they had ever read, but almost everyone agreed that you should go in blind. So I did.

I also just loved the cover. There is this little faun standing on a marble pillar playing his horn while water surrounds him. I initially thought he was a centaur, which he definitely is not, but either way he looked like he was having a great time. There was something so simple and strange about that illustration that made me want to know where he was. It ended up being a pretty perfect introduction to the book because so much of Piranesi is about looking at something strange and beautiful without immediately needing an explanation for it.

The book begins with Piranesi living in what he calls the House. As far as he knows, the House is the entire world. It consists of seemingly endless marble halls filled with statues. An ocean fills the lower levels, clouds gather in the upper levels, birds fly through the halls, and tides periodically flood portions of the House. Piranesi spends his days exploring, fishing, gathering seaweed and shellfish, studying the tides, documenting statues, and recording basically everything that happens to him in his journals. He believes only fifteen people have ever existed: himself, the Other, and thirteen dead people whose remains are scattered throughout the House.

Some of my favorite parts of the entire book happen before we really understand that there is even a mystery. Piranesi goes fishing at night because the moonlight helps him see the fish. He walks through halls that no one else seems to have visited, climbs staircases, studies the tides, and discovers new statues. Clarke makes these scenes where practically nothing happens feel gorgeous. There is something incredibly peaceful about following a person who can be completely occupied by looking at a statue or watching birds move through an empty building.

At one point, Piranesi remembers exploring while extremely hungry and entering halls filled with clouds and mist. The statues there are completely different from most of the ones he knows. They show horrible acts of violence: people fighting, people being attacked by centaurs and other creatures, and bodies being torn apart. Piranesi is genuinely frightened by them. He continues exploring anyway, can barely see where he is stepping, and eventually puts his foot forward only to discover that there is nothing beneath it. He nearly falls to his death and ends up clinging to one of the statues above this enormous drop. I loved that section. It was frightening, but Clarke somehow makes even the most dangerous parts of the House beautiful. Piranesi does not conquer the House in these scenes. He survives because he understands that he is small compared with it. That becomes increasingly important because he has a relationship with the House that nobody else seems to have.

Piranesi trusts the House completely. I mean completely. He believes that the House provides for him, protects him, and sometimes communicates with him. If he needs food and fish appear, he sees that as the generosity of the House. He studies the tides scientifically, but he also talks about them almost religiously. The House is not just the building in which he lives. He believes himself to be its “Beloved Child.” The birds are probably the strangest example of this. Piranesi observes their behavior constantly, and usually he understands that they are simply doing normal bird things. Sometimes, though, he believes their movements form messages. There is a moment when he watches a large gathering of birds moving among the statues and interprets what they are doing as a message about something approaching from far away, strange writing, and innocence being lost. Summarizing it makes Piranesi sound completely insane, but while I was reading it I somehow did not think he was.

That is one of the things I ended up loving most about him. Piranesi is incredibly scientific and incredibly spiritual at the exact same time. He measures everything. He records everything. He predicts the tides. He keeps indexes. He wants evidence. But none of that prevents him from believing that the world around him is alive with meaning.

The only other living person Piranesi regularly sees is a man he calls the Other. The Other is older, dresses nicely, brings modern objects with him, and occasionally uses a strange device that is very obviously a phone. Meanwhile, Piranesi has no shoes and is catching his dinner out of the ocean. There are about a thousand indications that these two men are not actually living under the same circumstances, but Piranesi never really questions it. He trusts the Other because, as far as he knows, the Other is his only friend.

The Other is obsessed with discovering what he calls the Great and Secret Knowledge. He believes that some ancient knowledge was lost from our world and that the House contains the secret to recovering it. He thinks this knowledge could give people extraordinary powers. Piranesi helps him search for it, but the two of them have completely different reasons for studying the House. Piranesi even becomes frustrated that the Other does not properly appreciate the statues. That difference ended up being one of the most important parts of the book for me. The Other looks at the House and asks what he can get from it, while Piranesi looks at the House and thinks that the fact that it exists is already enough.

Susanna Clarke has said that this contrast was deliberate. She describes the Other as someone who wants to exploit the House, whereas Piranesi wants to know it, not simply gather facts about it, but feel himself to be part of it. According to Clarke, Piranesi’s deeper knowledge comes from “seeing the beauty of the House” and feeling himself loved by it. I think that distinction explains almost everything that happens between them. Ketterley can enter another universe containing endless halls, impossible tides, and thousands of statues and somehow still come away disappointed because it will not give him superpowers. Piranesi can see two birds and have the best day of his life.

Things slowly start becoming more suspicious because Piranesi clearly has serious gaps in his memory. The Other calls him “Piranesi,” but Piranesi admits surprisingly early that he is fairly certain this is not actually his name. What is stranger is that he is not particularly bothered by this. You would think discovering that you have forgotten your own name would become your number-one priority. For Piranesi, it barely registers compared with understanding another hall or statue. Clarke has pointed this out herself. She said that Piranesi does not really need to recover some previous identity because what matters to him is being the Beloved Child of the House. That becomes much more interesting once we eventually learn who he used to be.

The journals are where everything really starts unraveling. Piranesi has spent years documenting his life, but he begins discovering references to things that he cannot remember writing. Some pages are missing entirely. At one point he discovers scraps of journal pages in birds’ nests, including frightening writing about someone wanting to kill someone else. Because the writing sounds nothing like the gentle Piranesi we know, it initially feels like evidence that something horrible happened in the House. Then Piranesi realizes that the writing is his. The journals basically become an external version of his memory. His brain has forgotten his past, but the person he used to be left evidence behind. Piranesi has to investigate himself.

Things become even stranger when another man suddenly appears in the House. Piranesi eventually calls him the Prophet. The Prophet immediately knows things that Piranesi does not and calls the Other by his real name: Valentine Ketterley. He claims that Ketterley was once his student and portrays him as basically an idiot who stole his ideas without ever properly understanding them. The Prophet also tells Piranesi that another person is searching for him. Piranesi then starts going through his old journals and searching their indexes for the strange names and words the Prophet has used. This was one of my favorite parts of the mystery. Piranesi does not simply read one entry that explains everything. He slowly reconstructs an entire world he has forgotten.

His old journals contain entries about Stanley Ovenden, Sylvia D’Agostino, James Ritter, Laurence Arne-Sayles, and others associated with this strange group of people interested in alternate worlds. At first Piranesi thinks these entries are almost nonsense because they describe things he has no memory of: universities, cities, police, interviews, jobs, families. He even wonders whether whoever wrote them was mentally ill. Eventually he realizes that these are not the journals of some crazy stranger. They were written by Matthew Rose Sorensen, and Matthew Rose Sorensen was him.

Before he became Piranesi, Matthew was a writer and journalist investigating Laurence Arne-Sayles. Arne-Sayles was an academic and occult figure who believed that ancient humans had once possessed a much deeper connection with reality and that this knowledge had somehow retreated into other worlds. He gathered students and followers around himself, including Valentine Ketterley. What I love about the journal entries is how different Matthew sounds from Piranesi. Matthew is skeptical, sarcastic, judgmental, and honestly a little pretentious. He thinks Arne-Sayles is basically a fraud and cannot understand how intelligent people have allowed themselves to believe this ridiculous stuff. He approaches the whole thing like a journalist trying to expose some strange intellectual cult.

That curiosity eventually leads him to Valentine Ketterley. Matthew goes to interview Ketterley because Ketterley had been one of Arne-Sayles’s students. Matthew is trying to understand how somebody could actually believe Arne-Sayles’s claims. Ketterley eventually tells Matthew that he can prove another world exists and offers to perform the ritual that will take him there. Matthew thinks the belief system is ridiculous, but he is curious enough to agree. That is the mistake that destroys his life. Ketterley performs the ritual, Matthew enters the House, and Ketterley leaves him there.

Over time the House affects Matthew’s memory. His memories of the outside world disappear. His family disappears from his mind. His work disappears. Eventually even the name Matthew Rose Sorensen disappears. What remains becomes Piranesi. It is such a weird transformation because Piranesi is in some ways a much kinder person than Matthew seems to have been. Matthew looks at Arne-Sayles and his followers and thinks, How can these people possibly believe something this stupid? Piranesi looks at a bird and wonders what it might be trying to tell him. Matthew judges. Piranesi observes.

I don’t think the book is saying that what happened to Matthew was somehow good. He was kidnapped. Six years of his life were stolen from him. Ketterley manipulated him and intentionally kept him ignorant. But Clarke also refuses to make Piranesi nothing more than a damaged version of Matthew. Piranesi became a real person too.

The journals make that especially clear. There is a great moment when Piranesi thinks about all of the strange entries he has read concerning Giussani, Ovenden, D’Agostino, and “poor James Ritter.” He had assumed that whoever wrote these things must have been mad because none of them fit his understanding of reality. He eventually realizes that Matthew was not mad at all. Matthew was simply living in another world with different rules. That realization works in both directions. Matthew was not insane for believing in London, universities, and police officers, but maybe Piranesi is not insane for believing that the House matters either.

The entry about James Ritter becomes particularly important. Ritter had once been one of Arne-Sayles’s victims. He disappeared for roughly two years and was eventually found imprisoned in Arne-Sayles’s house. After his rescue, he talked about living in a giant House filled with statues and enormous halls. Everyone in the outside world basically treated this as the delusion of a traumatized man. When Piranesi reads Ritter’s description, though, he recognizes the First Vestibule, the Minotaur statues, and even the place under the staircase where Ritter apparently used to sleep. Suddenly Piranesi has proof that someone else has lived in his world.

The First Vestibule itself had already been one of the strangest parts of the House. It contains eight huge Minotaur statues. Sometimes when Piranesi is there, he can smell things that do not seem to belong in the House, petrol, rain, things from the city, and hear distant sounds and voices. There seems to be a corridor between the Minotaurs, but whenever he leaves, his thoughts about it become strangely unclear. Eventually we understand that the Minotaurs mark the threshold between our world and the House. That is why ordinary sounds and smells occasionally bleed through there. It also explains the trash Piranesi once found underneath the staircase: James Ritter had actually lived there years earlier.

I like that Clarke uses Minotaurs at the entrance because the entire House is basically an enormous labyrinth. It also connects to one of Clarke’s major literary influences, Borges’s “The House of Asterion,” which retells the myth of the Minotaur from inside the labyrinth. Clarke has also talked about the influence of Giovanni Battista Piranesi’s enormous imaginary-prison engravings. The House looks like something that should be a prison, yet Piranesi experiences it as beautiful and almost limitless.

The dead make the House even stranger. Piranesi knows the remains of thirteen people and feels an almost automatic responsibility to care for them. He gathers their bones, brings them offerings, leaves them food and water lilies, and talks to them. He does not know who most of them were, but that does not seem to matter to him. The strangest is the Folded-Up Child, the skeleton of a young girl wearing a necklace made from coral and fishbones. At first I kept waiting for the book to reveal who she was, but it never does.

Apparently that is intentional. Clarke has said that she personally knows which dead person is which, but she left many of those answers out because Piranesi himself could never realistically discover them. I actually love that decision. Not every mystery in the House exists for us to solve. Piranesi does not need to know the little girl’s biography before deciding that she deserves to be cared for. I also wondered whether Matthew might have known any of these people before they died, considering he had only been trapped in the House for six years. I don’t think he did. Even Piranesi later tells Raphael that he believes all thirteen were already dead when he arrived, although his memory is obviously unreliable enough that he cannot be completely certain. James Ritter, importantly, is not one of the dead. He escaped the House decades earlier and is still alive.

There is something really beautiful about Piranesi caring so much about forgotten people while he himself is becoming a forgotten person. Matthew Rose Sorensen’s family does not know where he is. Most of Matthew’s own memories are disappearing. Yet Piranesi is spending his days making sure that strangers who died long before him are not completely abandoned. Maybe some of that is Matthew surviving inside him. Maybe it is something Piranesi developed by living in the House. There is even evidence that people before him had done the same thing: the ancient Fish-Leather Man’s bones had already been carefully gathered and preserved by somebody else. Caring for the dead almost seems like a tradition that passes between the House’s inhabitants even when their names and stories disappear.

The Prophet eventually turns out to be Laurence Arne-Sayles himself. This means that Piranesi has actually met the man Matthew was once investigating without knowing who he is. Arne-Sayles knows far more about the House than Ketterley does and seems amused by the fact that his former student is still obsessively hunting for the Great and Secret Knowledge. Meanwhile Ketterley becomes increasingly threatening. He tells Piranesi that another person, whom he calls “16,” is coming and that this person is extremely dangerous. He says speaking to 16 could destroy Piranesi’s mind and eventually talks openly about killing them. Piranesi believes him.

At first this reminded me of Stockholm syndrome, but I do not really think that is what makes the situation disturbing. Ketterley’s real power is that he controls Piranesi’s understanding of reality. If you cannot remember your family, your past, or even your own name, and one person appears to be the only other person in existence who understands what is going on, why wouldn’t you believe him? Sixteen eventually turns out to be Sarah Raphael, a police officer investigating Matthew Rose Sorensen’s disappearance. She has not come to hurt Piranesi. She has come to bring him home.

Raphael starts leaving messages for him, including the question of whether he is Matthew Rose Sorensen. Just seeing that name begins triggering something inside him. Piranesi starts piecing together the missing journal pages and finally understands what Ketterley did to him. There is a moment around here that I really liked where Piranesi almost imagines Matthew as another person living inside him. Matthew is frightened and overwhelmed by what happened, and Piranesi essentially comforts him. Instead of immediately trying to become Matthew again, Piranesi takes care of Matthew in the same way he takes care of practically everyone else. That really changed how I saw the identity question in the book. Piranesi does not simply discover that his real name was Matthew. He discovers that Matthew was someone he used to be.

Ketterley eventually realizes Raphael has entered the House, and at the same time Piranesi predicts that a huge conjunction of tides is about to cause a catastrophic flood. Everything comes together at once. Raphael is trying to reach Piranesi, Ketterley comes intending to kill her, and the House begins filling with water. Piranesi survives because he understands the House better than anyone else. He knows the statues. He knows the halls. Most importantly, he understands the tides. Ketterley has spent years trying to exploit this world while barely bothering to know it, and the House ultimately kills him. Piranesi, who respected it enough to study it, knows how to survive.

Even then, Piranesi tries to save Ketterley. That might be the clearest demonstration of who he has become. He now knows this man kidnapped him, lied to him, manipulated him for six years, damaged his journals, threatened Raphael, and is literally trying to murder them. Piranesi still tells him how to get to safety, but Ketterley drowns anyway.

Afterward Raphael tells Piranesi the truth. Matthew Rose Sorensen disappeared six years earlier. His family is still alive. They have missed him. The outside world still exists and he can return to it. The obvious ending would be for Matthew to get rescued, recover his memories, reunite with his family, and become himself again, but Clarke refuses to do that.

The person who leaves the House is not Matthew Rose Sorensen anymore, but he is not completely Piranesi either. He eventually thinks of himself almost as a third person containing both of them. He allows his family to call him Matthew because he understands that their Matthew disappeared and that this comforts them, but internally the identity does not completely fit. I absolutely loved that because you cannot lose six years of yourself and then just hit undo.

The book also refuses to turn the House into something evil once Piranesi understands what happened. That distinction is really important. The House did not kidnap Matthew; Ketterley did. Piranesi continues visiting the House after returning to our world. Raphael teaches him how to find the path in and out, so he can now move between both places freely. Instead of being trapped in one world, he finally has access to both.

Then he does something I found incredibly sad and beautiful: he finds James Ritter. Ritter is now living in Manchester, decades removed from the place that everyone else assumed was part of his mental breakdown. Piranesi approaches him and tells him that he has come to take him back. When they enter the House and the Minotaur statues rise around them again, Ritter starts crying from happiness. He immediately goes underneath the staircase to the exact place where he used to sleep and begs Piranesi to let him stay. Piranesi refuses because Ritter does not know how to survive there by himself, but he promises that he can bring him back whenever he wants.

That scene might be one of the most important in the entire ending because it proves that Piranesi’s attachment to the House is not simply the result of him being uniquely delusional. Ritter was traumatized by what Arne-Sayles did to him. He was rescued. He lived in our world for decades. And when he finally sees the House again, he cries because some part of him has been missing it the entire time. That complicates the idea of “rescue” in such an interesting way. Ritter needed to be rescued from Arne-Sayles. Piranesi needed to be rescued from Ketterley. But neither of them necessarily needed to be rescued from the House.

That makes even more sense after reading Clarke’s interviews. Clarke dealt with years of severe illness and isolation while writing the novel, and she has talked about becoming interested in the idea that being physically confined does not necessarily mean your experience of life has to become empty. She wanted Piranesi to experience an intense relationship with the limited world immediately around him. I think that is why all those early passages about fishing, statues, birds, shells, clouds, and tides matter so much. They are not just pretty filler before the mystery gets interesting. They are the point. Piranesi’s physical world has become unbelievably restricted, yet his experience of it somehow becomes enormous.

Clarke also connects Piranesi’s worldview to the idea of “original participation,” this sense that human beings are not detached observers looking at a dead external world but participants in something larger. That helped me understand why Piranesi’s almost religious trust in the House never completely disappears once he returns to normal life. He was wrong about some things. The birds might not literally be sending him messages. The House may not consciously decide to put fish where he needs them. But the alternative the novel gives us is not necessarily better. Ketterley understands more about modern reality than Piranesi does, but he seems almost incapable of seeing anything unless it can benefit him.

That is why I think the Great and Secret Knowledge becomes almost a joke by the end. Ketterley spends years searching for the secret hidden inside the House, while Piranesi eventually understands that searching for some final secret can actually make you stop seeing what is directly in front of you. If the halls and statues exist only as clues in a puzzle, then once you solve the puzzle they become useless. Piranesi refuses to look at the House that way. The House itself is the extraordinary thing.

The statues also make more sense to me after reading Clarke’s explanation of them. She has said that she did not intend every statue to have one fixed symbolic meaning. She wanted them to work more like images in dreams: things that feel significant even when you cannot completely explain why. That is probably why trying to decode each individual statue never really works. They are not clues in the traditional sense. They form a kind of visual language for the House, and that makes the ending even better.

Back in our world, the narrator starts seeing people who remind him of statues in the House. The two worlds begin overlapping in the way he looks at them. At first I thought this was simply Piranesi missing the House, but I think it is more than that. Piranesi learned how to look. Matthew Rose Sorensen lived in the normal world and became a skeptical journalist investigating people he thought were fools. Piranesi lived in a magical world and learned to notice tiny differences in birds, tides, statues, clouds, and light. The person who exists at the end contains both perspectives, and he can function in our world without completely losing the way Piranesi saw things.

That is what makes the final sentence hit so hard. The book begins with a man who thinks the House is the entire world. It ends with someone who realizes that the beauty he found in the House was never necessarily restricted to it. I don’t think Piranesi is saying that ignorance is better than knowledge. Piranesi is probably the most obsessive recorder of information in the entire book. The distinction is between knowing something because you love it and knowing something because you want to own it. Ketterley wants knowledge so that the world will become useful to him, while Piranesi wants knowledge so that he can become closer to the world. In the end, the person who wanted power gets basically nothing, while the person who never wanted power gets something much more interesting: the ability to find meaning almost everywhere.

I went into Piranesi because Jack Edwards gave it to Matt Damon and Matt Damon looked slightly disappointed about not getting Anne Hathaway’s book. I expected some weird fantasy mystery about a guy walking through an endless marble building. For a while, that is basically what it was, and I loved those parts more than I expected to. Then it became a story about kidnapping, memory, identity, loneliness, knowledge, manipulation, faith, trauma, and what actually makes someone who they are.

I think what will stick with me most, though, is something much simpler. Piranesi spends most of the book surrounded by things he cannot completely understand, and he does not respond by trying to conquer all of them. He pays attention to them. Maybe that is why he ends up understanding the House better than anyone else ever did. Also, I still love the little faun on the cover. So real.
